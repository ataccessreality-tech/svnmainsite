How to Choose a Property Location Beyond the Map Pin

A practical method for comparing routes, services, neighbourhood conditions and proposed infrastructure.

Location · 2 min read · 11 October 2026

In this article
  1. Define what the location needs to do
  2. Test the journey from the actual entrance
  3. Check services and site conditions
  4. Separate present value from future proposals
  5. Build a useful comparison

Define what the location needs to do

The best location starts with the buyer, not a universal list of famous neighbourhoods. Write down the places you need to reach, how often you travel, who will live there and when you expect to use the property. Separate essential requirements from preferences.

A family living there every day may place schools, healthcare and commuting first. Someone buying a plot to build later still needs to understand current access, services and holding costs. Compare each candidate against the same requirements.

Test the journey from the actual entrance

Use the exact entrance as your starting point. A straight-line distance can hide a long diversion, unsuitable road or restricted access. Record the route, time of day and travel time to each essential destination. Try an alternative route where available.

Observe the last part of the journey as carefully as the main highway. Ask whether legal access is documented, who maintains the connecting road and whether the route remains usable in wet weather. A visible track is not enough to establish an access right.

Check services and site conditions

Ask how water reaches the property, whether individual electricity connections are available and where drainage flows. Find out who supplies and maintains these services and what you will pay. A pipeline or transformer on site may represent only one stage of the work.

Look at neighbouring occupation, traffic, noise, lighting and low areas. Visit at different times where possible. If flood, slope, soil or coastal conditions raise concerns, get an appropriate technical assessment rather than relying on a short sales visit.

Separate present value from future proposals

Create two columns: available now and proposed. For a new airport, highway or commercial centre, record an official source, location, current status and any confirmed access arrangement. If a completion date cannot be verified, leave it unconfirmed.

Test whether you would still consider the property if the proposed work took longer than expected. Future infrastructure can inform a decision, but it should not carry the entire case for a purchase or be treated as a guarantee of resale value.

Build a useful comparison

Score each location consistently for essential travel, legal access, services, site conditions and affordability. Choose your own priorities and keep a serious unresolved legal or technical issue outside the score; a high average cannot cancel it.

Use the downloadable scorecard for two or three shortlisted locations. Add dates and evidence beside observations so the comparison remains useful after the excitement of a site visit has passed.
